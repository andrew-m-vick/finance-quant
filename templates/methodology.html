Methodology

The accounting rules, the statistics, and the limits of each tool, written so a reviewer can check the work.

Part I covers the accounting tools. Part II covers the earlier quantitative personal-finance models.

Part I · Accounting tools

Ground rules for all three

  • Exact decimal arithmetic. Every dollar amount is parsed into Python's Decimal and rounded to the cent (round-half-up). Binary floating point can't represent 0.10 exactly, and a ledger that's off by $0.01 from float drift is still a ledger that doesn't tie.
  • Stateless. Pasted data is processed in memory and discarded. There is no database and no logging of inputs.
  • Tested invariants. The test suite asserts accounting identities (debits = credits, A = L + E, a post-closing TB that balances, adjusted bank = adjusted book) rather than just "the code runs."

1. Trial balance → financial statements

Input is an adjusted trial balance: adjusting entries (accruals, deferrals, depreciation, bad-debt estimates) are already posted, and Retained Earnings still shows its beginning balance, because closing hasn't happened yet. Each account carries a category that fixes its normal balance and where it is presented.

1a. Statements produced

  • Multi-step income statement. Net sales (gross revenue less contra-revenue such as sales returns) → gross profit → operating income → income before taxes → net income. Non-operating items such as interest are kept below operating income so the operating margin reflects the core business.
  • Statement of retained earnings. Ending RE = Beginning RE + Net income − Dividends.
  • Classified balance sheet. Current vs. noncurrent assets and liabilities; contra-assets (accumulated depreciation, allowance for doubtful accounts) are shown as deductions so net book value is visible. Equity uses the ending retained earnings from the statement above. That linkage is what makes the balance sheet balance.
  • Closing entries in the standard four-step sequence: close revenues to Income Summary, close expenses to Income Summary, close Income Summary to Retained Earnings, close Dividends to Retained Earnings. A post-closing trial balance then confirms only permanent accounts remain and still balance.

1b. Finding errors in a trial balance that doesn't balance

These are the manual techniques taught in Intermediate Accounting, automated:

  • Difference ÷ 9. Swapping two digits (24 → 42) or sliding a decimal (45 → 450) always changes an amount by a multiple of 9, because 10ᵃ ≡ 1 (mod 9). When the difference is divisible by 9, the tool tries every two-digit swap of every balance and lists those that would make the TB tie, limited to swaps in the direction that would fix the imbalance.
  • Difference ÷ 2. An amount posted to the wrong side moves the totals by twice its value. Any account equal to half the difference is listed.
  • Difference = an account balance. Points to an omitted or double-counted account.
  • Abnormal balances. An expense with a credit balance or an asset with a credit balance is flagged even when the TB balances, because two offsetting errors can still tie.

1c. Ratios

Current ratio, working capital, debt-to-equity, gross/operating/net margin, ROA, ROE, and effective tax rate. ROA and ROE use ending balances because a single trial balance has no prior period. With comparative data, average balances are the better denominator.

Not yet included: the statement of cash flows, which needs a prior-period balance sheet for the indirect method. Comparative trial balance input is the natural next step.

2. Bank reconciliation

The goal is to prove that the general-ledger cash balance and the bank's balance describe the same cash once timing differences and errors are explained. It is one of the most basic cash controls, and auditors check that it is done monthly and reviewed by someone other than the preparer.

2a. Matching, strongest evidence first

  1. Check number. Same check number on both sides. If the amounts differ, it's a recording error. The bank's cleared amount is treated as correct, since it's what actually left the account. Generic tags like "DEP" are ignored; only references containing digits count.
  2. Exact amount within a date window (default 7 days; the bank may post up to 2 days before the book date). Ties go to the nearest date.
  3. Transposition scan. Leftover pairs whose amounts are digit permutations of each other within the window (e.g. 492.00 vs 429.00).

2b. Classifying what's left

Bank side                              Book side
  Balance per bank                       Balance per books
+ Deposits in transit                  + Bank credits not recorded (interest, collections)
− Outstanding checks                   − Bank debits not recorded (fees, NSF checks)
                                       ± Error corrections
= Adjusted bank balance        ==      = Adjusted book balance

Only book-side items get adjusting journal entries; bank-side items clear on their own next month. Bank items are mapped to accounts by keyword: NSF → Accounts Receivable (the customer still owes), interest → Interest Revenue, service charge → Bank Service Charges Expense. Anything unrecognized goes to Suspense, flagged for investigation rather than guessed.

3. Audit data analytics

3a. Benford's Law

In many naturally occurring financial datasets, the leading digit d appears with probability: 

P(d) = log₁₀(1 + 1/d)      →  1: 30.1%,  2: 17.6%,  …  9: 4.6%
P(d₁d₂) = log₁₀(1 + 1/d₁d₂)  for the first two digits, 10–99

Fabricated numbers tend not to follow this, because people over-use middle digits and cluster amounts below control thresholds. The first-two-digits test is the more useful audit test: it has 90 bins instead of 9, so it pinpoints which amounts are over-represented. In the sample file, the first-digit test rates the population "acceptable," and only the first-two-digit test exposes the spike at 47–49.

3b. Measuring conformity

  • Mean absolute deviation (MAD) is the headline verdict, judged against Nigrini's critical values (first digit: ≤ 0.006 close, ≤ 0.012 acceptable, ≤ 0.015 marginal, above that nonconformity; first-two: 0.0012 / 0.0018 / 0.0022). MAD is used because chi-square's power grows with sample size: with thousands of records it rejects almost any real dataset over trivial deviations.
  • Chi-square is still reported. For the first-digit test (8 df) the p-value is computed exactly. For the first-two test (89 df) it uses the Wilson–Hilferty approximation.
  • Per-bin z-statistics with a continuity correction, z = (|p̂ − p| − 1/2N) / √(p(1−p)/N). Bins with |z| > 1.96 that are over-represented are flagged. With 90 bins, about two spikes are expected at that threshold by chance alone (90 × 2.5%), so an isolated spike matters less than a cluster of adjacent ones, like 47–48–49 in the sample.
  • Amounts under $10 are excluded, following Nigrini: they are dominated by fees and small adjustments that distort the low digits.

3c. Approval-limit and duplicate tests

  • Just-below-limit clustering. Compares the count of payments in the 10% band below the approval limit with the 10% band above. Benford implies the lower band should hold only slightly more, log(1/0.9) / log(1.1) ≈ 1.11×. A ratio above twice that (with at least 10 items) is flagged, along with which vendors make up the band. This is the classic pattern of invoice splitting to avoid a second approval.
  • Duplicate invoices. Same vendor and same invoice number paid more than once; the overpayment exposure is every payment after the first.
  • Repeated amounts. The same vendor paid an identical amount (≥ $100) multiple times. It is often legitimate (retainers, rent) but worth agreeing to a contract.

Benford's Law is a risk-assessment tool, not proof of fraud. It tells an auditor where to direct substantive testing. It doesn't apply to assigned numbers (check numbers, invoice numbers), amounts with built-in minimums or maximums, or small populations.

Part II · Quantitative personal-finance models

4. The Stress Test

The stress test is a heuristic scorecard, not a statistical model. It runs the user's balance sheet through five adverse scenarios and grades the response on a weighted rubric.

4a. Scenarios

  • Job loss runway. Months of fixed expenses plus 50% of variable expenses that liquid savings can cover with zero income. Thresholds: 12+ months strong, 6–12 adequate, 3–6 weak, <3 critical. The 12-month bar is aggressive by mainstream advice (3–6 months is the usual guidance), but it's the level at which you can realistically survive a bad labor market for a technical role without forced fire sales.
  • 30% market drawdown. Applies a 30% loss to invested assets. This approximates the S&P 500's behavior in 2008 (−37%), 2020 COVID crash (−34% intraday), and 2022 tech-led correction (−25%). Marked informational because you can't "fix" being invested — the goal is to confirm your liquid cushion exists outside the drawdown.
  • $5K emergency expense. A round-number shock that approximates a medical deductible, a transmission, or a family obligation. Measures whether one bad week empties your cushion.
  • Debt service ratio. Monthly debt payments ÷ monthly income. Thresholds mirror mortgage-underwriting bands: <15% strong, <28% adequate (the front-end DTI ceiling used by many lenders), <40% weak (back-end DTI ceiling), ≥40% critical.
  • 5% inflation for 2 years. Compounds expenses at 5% annually while growing income at 2% — modeling the real-world lag between prices and wages. This was the lived experience of 2021–2023, where CPI ran ~5–9% and wage growth trailed.

4b. Scoring

Each scenario maps to a 0–100 severity score. The final resilience score is a weighted average: 

score = 0.35·runway + 0.20·emergency + 0.20·DTI + 0.15·inflation + 0.10·drawdown

Job loss gets the heaviest weight because it's the most common catastrophic financial event for working-age adults. Market drawdown gets the lowest because, unlike the others, you don't act on it — you wait.

5. The Monte Carlo Retirement Simulator

5a. Model

Portfolio balance is propagated year-by-year with contributions at the start of each year and stochastic returns drawn from a log-normal distribution: 

B_{t+1} = (B_t + C) · exp(Z_t)
Z_t ~ Normal(ln(1+μ) − σ²/2, σ²)

Where B is balance, C is annual contribution, μ is the expected arithmetic return (i.e. the average of annual (1+R)−1 outcomes), and σ is the standard deviation of log-returns. Drawing Z_t from this shifted distribution makes E[1 + R] = 1 + μ — so the long-run expected portfolio value compounds at (1+μ)^T, not exp(μT). (Those differ noticeably over decades.)

5b. Why geometric, not arithmetic, returns

This is the most common mistake in retirement projections. If you project growth at the arithmetic mean return μ, you will systematically overstate outcomes. The reason is that compounding is multiplicative, not additive: a +50% year followed by a −50% year is not flat — it's −25%.

The relationship between the arithmetic mean μ and the geometric mean g of log-normal returns is: 

g ≈ μ − σ²/2

So for an equity portfolio with μ = 10% and σ = 16%, the geometric return is ≈ 8.7%. Over 40 years, that 1.3% drag compounds to roughly a 40% reduction in terminal wealth. The engine draws log-returns from Normal(μ − σ²/2, σ²) so that the expected ending balance is consistent with μ while individual paths still capture volatility drag.

5c. Sequence of returns risk

Two retirees with identical average returns can end up in wildly different places depending on when the bad years hit. Bad years early — when the portfolio is still small and contributions dominate — are survivable. Bad years late — when the portfolio is large and you are about to or already drawing down — are not.

This simulator captures sequence risk implicitly: each of the 10,000 paths has a unique return sequence. The 10th-percentile path is almost always a path where the worst years clustered near the end. That is why the fan chart fans out dramatically in the final decade — outcomes diverge based on sequence, not just mean.

5d. Inflation and real vs. nominal

Nominal dollars at retirement are deflated at the user's inflation rate to produce real (today's) dollars: 

real = nominal / (1 + i)^years

The real column of the percentiles table is the honest number — it represents purchasing power in today's terms. Nominal figures are larger and look more impressive, but they are quoted in dollars that buy less.

5e. The 4% rule context

A common way to convert a goal number into retirement income is the 4% safe-withdrawal rule (Bengen 1994, Trinity Study 1998): a retiree withdrawing 4% of their starting balance, adjusted for inflation, had a ≥95% historical success rate over 30-year horizons on a 60/40 portfolio. So a $2M target implies roughly $80,000/year of pretax inflation-adjusted income. The rule has well-documented weaknesses (it's backtested on US data, assumes a specific asset mix, and ignores sequence risk at the start of retirement) but it remains a useful sanity check.

6. What the quantitative models can't tell you

  • Fat tails. Both tools assume returns are roughly normal. Real markets have fatter tails — extreme events happen more often than a Gaussian predicts. The 10th-percentile outcome is probably optimistic about how bad the bad cases really are.
  • Stationary distributions. The simulator assumes μ and σ are constant across the horizon. Regimes change — interest rates, valuations, and correlations shift over decades. Recalibrate periodically.
  • Correlated shocks. The stress test treats scenarios independently, but in practice a job loss and a market drawdown often happen together (2008, 2020). Your true resilience is lower than any single scenario suggests.
  • Behavior. The biggest risk to any long-horizon plan is the investor selling at the bottom. No model captures that.
